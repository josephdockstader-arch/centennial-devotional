The Author of Salvation

Every Story Points to Him

And being made perfect, he became the author of eternal salvation unto all them that obey him.

Hebrews 5:9

A featured devotional: one talk, given by an invited speaker — to be announced.

About this name

Hebrews calls Jesus the captain of salvation, the author of eternal salvation, and the author and finisher of faith. These titles describe his work in bringing people to God. Hebrews places his suffering and obedience at the center of that work and presents him as the high priest who can help those who are tempted.

The Old Testament supplies much of the language through which the New Testament explains Christ. Sacrifice, deliverance from Egypt, priesthood, kingship, and redemption each contribute something different. Moses 5 explicitly connects Adam's sacrifices with the Only Begotten. Paul connects the Passover with Christ.

The word behind author at Hebrews 5:9 is aitios, which is ordinarily translated cause. Hebrews 12:2 uses a different word, archegos, which is closer to leader or founder. This program keeps author for the discussion, and speakers should know that the two verses are not using the same word. Studying these connections gives this devotional its task: to explain how an earlier event or institution prepares readers to understand what Jesus accomplishes.

Suggested reading

This is a featured devotional, so no cohort is assigned and nothing here is required of anyone. It is for students who want to come to the talk already knowing the texts behind it.

  • Hebrews 2:5–18; 4:14–5:10; 12:1–3.
  • Moses 5:1–12; 6:48–63.
  • Genesis 22:1–19; Jacob 4:4–7.
  • Exodus 12:1–28; 1 Corinthians 5:6–8; 10:1–4.
  • Numbers 21:4–9; John 3:14–17; Alma 33:19–23.
  • Mosiah 13:27–35; D&C 84:19–27.
  • John Taylor, Mediation and Atonement, chapters VII and XIII, on sacrifice and Passover.
  • Ruth 1–4 with Leviticus 25:23–28, 47–55: kinship and redemption.
  • Esther 4–7: intercession and the risk of approaching a king.
  • 1 Samuel 17 with Ezekiel 34:20–24: deliverance and the promised Davidic shepherd.
  • Jonah 1–4 with Matthew 12:38–41: the sign Jesus names.
  • 1 Kings 18:17–39; 2 Kings 17:5–23; 25:1–21: covenant loyalty, exile, and the need for deliverance.

Questions for study

  1. Compare "captain," "author," and "finisher" in their passages in Hebrews. What aspects of Christ's work does each title emphasize? Which features disappear if "author" is understood only as someone who writes a story?
  2. Hebrews says Christ was made perfect through suffering. What does the surrounding argument suggest "perfect" means here? How can this be reconciled with his being without sin?
  3. In Moses 5, Adam offers sacrifice before he understands its purpose. What changes when the angel explains it? What place do obedience and understanding each have in worship?
  4. Compare Genesis 22 with Jacob 4:5. Which details support Jacob's reading of Abraham and Isaac as a similitude of the Father and Son? Where do the two events differ, and what follows from the differences?
  5. What does Passover deliver Israel from? How do Paul and Taylor use that deliverance to explain Christ? Does the image emphasize rescue, sacrifice, belonging to a people, or several of these?
  6. Why does Alma use the account of the brass serpent to teach faith? What makes the commanded response difficult despite its simplicity?
  7. Choose one of the additional narratives below. Develop a reading of its relationship to Christ, then compare it with a connection explicitly made in the required texts. What makes an interpretation persuasive?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Which of these accounts best expresses something you need from a Savior: deliverance, forgiveness, guidance, or help with weakness? What in the account helps you understand that need?
  2. Hebrews puts obedience and suffering inside the work of opening the way. If that is right, what does it ask of the people who follow him? Where does your own week answer that, and where does it not?
