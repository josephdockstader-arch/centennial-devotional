The Messiah

They All Foretold Him

He first findeth his own brother Simon, and saith unto him, We have found the Messias, which is, being interpreted, the Christ.

John 1:41

About this name

Andrew tells Simon that he has found the Messiah. John translates the title as Christ: the Anointed One. Israel's scriptures associate anointing with kings and priests, and Isaiah describes an anointed messenger bringing good tidings to the afflicted.

Jesus reads that passage in Nazareth and announces its fulfillment. His hearers then object as he speaks about God's dealings with a widow in Sidon and a Syrian named Naaman. The encounter raises questions about what people expected from the Messiah and whom they believed his blessings should reach. Isaiah's suffering servant, Micah's ruler from Bethlehem, and the Book of Mormon's accounts of the coming Redeemer provide several texts against which to test those expectations.

Required reading for every speaker

  • John 1:35–51; Luke 4:16–30.
  • Isaiah 11:1–10; 42:1–9; 52:13–53:12; 61:1–3; Micah 5:2–5.
  • Exodus 28:41; 1 Samuel 16:1–13; 1 Kings 19:15–16.
  • 1 Nephi 11:13–33; Alma 7:7–13; Helaman 14:1–13.
  • D&C 19:1–4, 15–19; Moses 7:53–55.
  • John Taylor, Mediation and Atonement, chapter II, collecting Old Testament witnesses of Christ.
  • 3 Nephi 1:4–21; Matthew 2:1–12; Luke 24:13–35.
  • 1 Kings 17:8–24; 2 Kings 5:1–19, the accounts Jesus invokes at Nazareth.
  • Bible Dictionary entries "Anointed One" and "Messiah."

Questions for research and discussion

  1. Examine the anointings in Exodus, 1 Samuel, and 1 Kings. What responsibilities accompany each office? Which of those responsibilities appear in the passages about Jesus?
  2. Compare Isaiah 11, 42, and 53. What expectations might each passage produce? How could people who accepted all three still disagree about what the Messiah would do?
  3. Luke 4:22–29 records a change in the synagogue's response. What happens between their first reaction and their attempt to kill Jesus? Why are the widow and Naaman so important to the exchange?
  4. Identify who speaks and who suffers in Isaiah 53. How does the speaker's understanding of the suffering change? Which lines most strongly support the Christian reading of this passage?
  5. Compare Isaiah 53 with Alma 7:11–13. How does each text explain the relationship between Christ's suffering and his ability to help others? What questions does one address that the other does not?
  6. Micah identifies Bethlehem as small among Judah's communities. How does that description contribute to the promised ruler's significance? Compare it with the selection of David in 1 Samuel 16.
  7. Samuel names a time for the sign of Christ's birth. Read his prophecy beside 3 Nephi 1:4–21. How do a promised date and an approaching threat change the demands placed on believers?
  8. Select one passage Taylor uses in chapter II. Does his surrounding argument help explain the passage, extend its application, or both? What would someone need to understand about its original setting to assess his reading?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Think of something you waited for and then found different from what you expected. How did you respond? How might that experience help you understand either Andrew's welcome or the resistance in Nazareth?
  2. The synagogue agreed with him until he named who the blessing would reach. Who would you have trouble seeing him bless?
