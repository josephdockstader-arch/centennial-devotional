The Lord

Prepare Ye the Way

The voice of him that crieth in the wilderness, Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make straight in the desert a highway for our God.

Isaiah 40:3

About this name

Isaiah calls for a way to be prepared for the Lord through the wilderness. The Gospels apply that passage to John the Baptist. John preaches repentance, baptizes, confronts rulers and religious leaders, and announces the coming of someone greater than himself.

Luke begins John's history in the temple with Zacharias and later places John in the wilderness. His ministry carries the authority of a divine commission into a setting outside the temple establishment. Joseph Smith's teachings about John and the account of his appearance in 1829 extend the study of that commission. John's work raises a practical question about religious leadership: how does someone exercise real authority while directing people's allegiance toward Christ?

The title itself needs care. In the Old Testament the English LORD in small capitals stands for the divine name; in the New Testament the Greek is kurios, which the Septuagint also used where the divine name stood. This program treats such titles as offices held rather than as personal names, so the question to ask of any passage is which office is acting and who holds it at that point.

Required reading for every speaker

  • Luke 1:5–25, 39–45, 57–80; 3:1–22; 7:18–35.
  • Isaiah 40:1–11; Malachi 3:1–4; 4:5–6.
  • Matthew 3:1–17; John 1:19–34; 3:22–36.
  • 2 Kings 1:1–8; 2 Nephi 31:4–13.
  • D&C 13; 84:26–28; Joseph Smith—History 1:68–74.
  • Joseph Smith, "The Mission of John the Baptist," chapter 6.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. Compare Isaiah 40 and Malachi 3. What needs to happen before the Lord's coming in each passage? How does John's ministry address those needs?
  2. Research the similarities between Elijah's description in 2 Kings 1 and John's in Matthew 3. How do Luke 1:17 and John 1:21 help explain both the connection and John's denial that he is Elijah?
  3. In Luke 3, what does John ask the crowd, tax collectors, and soldiers to do? What definition of repentance emerges from these different instructions?
  4. John's disciples are concerned that people are going to Jesus. How does John answer them in John 3? What might a leader have to give up to answer the same way?
  5. What question does John send from prison, and what evidence does Jesus send back? Compare the answer with Isaiah 35:3–6 and 61:1–3. Would it answer every concern a prisoner might have?
  6. Joseph Smith explains John's greatness through his commission and authority. How does this reading affect the meaning of Jesus's tribute in Luke 7? What measures of religious success does it challenge?
  7. Compare John's work in the Gospels with his commission in Joseph Smith—History and D&C 13. What continues across the two settings? Why would repentance and baptism be preparatory in both?
  8. John confronts Herod in Luke 3:19–20. How does that confrontation belong to his work of preparing the way? What tensions arise when the call to repentance reaches those who hold power?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Who helped prepare you to recognize or receive something good? Did that person get to see what came of the help? How does remembering that person affect your understanding of John's work?
  2. John sent his own disciples to Jesus. Describe a time you handed something you had built to someone else. What was hard about it?

Additional research readings

  • Isaiah 35:1–10; Matthew 11:2–19; Mark 6:14–29.
  • JST Matthew 11:13–15 in the Bible appendix; compare its treatment of John's mission with Matthew 11.
