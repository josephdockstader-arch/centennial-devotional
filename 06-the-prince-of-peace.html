The Prince of Peace

Unto You Is Born

For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.

Isaiah 9:6

About this name

Isaiah's promise of a child comes after images of oppression and war. The promised peace includes government, judgment, and justice. Luke announces peace at Jesus's birth, while Matthew describes Herod's violence and the family's flight to Egypt. The title therefore raises a question the birth narratives themselves do not let us avoid: what does Christ's coming change in a world where violence continues?

The witnesses at his birth approach that question from different positions. Shepherds receive an announcement, wise men seek a king, Simeon anticipates both salvation and opposition, and Anna speaks to those waiting for redemption. Their responses supply the center of this week's study.

Required reading for every speaker

  • Isaiah 9:1–7; 11:1–9; Micah 5:2–5.
  • Luke 2:1–40; Matthew 2:1–23.
  • 3 Nephi 1:4–26; Mosiah 15:14–18.
  • Galatians 4:1–7; Ephesians 2:11–22; John 14:25–31.
  • D&C 19:23; 59:23; Moses 7:18–21.
  • Parley P. Pratt, Key to the Science of Theology (1855), chapter I, including his treatment of revelation surrounding Christ's birth.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. What conditions define peace in Isaiah 9 and 11? How does that account compare with the peace promised in John 14 and D&C 59? Are these different promises or different aspects of one promise?
  2. Compare the shepherds, wise men, Simeon, and Anna. How does each learn about the child, and what does each do in response? What forms of preparation can you identify?
  3. Research the scriptural background of Simeon's words in Luke 2:29–35. Why does his blessing include both light for the Gentiles and a warning of opposition?
  4. Matthew quotes or invokes earlier scripture throughout chapter 2. Trace one connection to its earlier passage. How does it shape Matthew's account of the child's identity and danger?
  5. In 3 Nephi 1, the sign saves believers from an immediate threat. How does this deliverance compare with what happens to families under Herod? What questions arise when divine deliverance takes different forms?
  6. Pratt places the birth witnesses within his larger account of revelation. Which examples in Luke and Matthew best support his argument? What distinguishes receiving a revelation from understanding everything it will mean?
  7. Ephesians 2 describes peace through the removal of hostility between peoples. What obligations follow for a community that calls Jesus the Prince of Peace? Use a specific division to examine what reconciliation would require.

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. What kind of peace do you most hope Christ can bring? Which person or promise in these readings helps you put that hope into words?
  2. Is there a disagreement you are keeping alive right now? What would it take to end it, and why have you not ended it?

Additional research readings

  • Hosea 11:1–4; Jeremiah 31:15–17; Matthew 2:13–18.
  • Brigham Young, "Developing Christlike Attitudes toward Others," chapter 30.
