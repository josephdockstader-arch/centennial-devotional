The Word

That Ye Might Believe

And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth.

John 1:14

About this name

John begins with the Word who was with God, who was God, and through whom all things were made. When he says that the Word became flesh, he connects the life of Jesus with the creation of the world. D&C 93 returns to John's testimony and connects it with Christ's growth from grace to grace.

John says all things were made by him. This program does not read that as hands-on creation. It reads it as the contract and the authority. The agreement between the Father and the Son had to be in place before this creation could be contemplated, and the office the Son holds carries authority in creation.

This cohort also studies the records through which we know Christ's mortal ministry. Matthew opens with a genealogy, Mark with prophetic preparation, and Luke with an explanation of his investigation. John states that he selected signs so readers might believe. Their choices affect what readers learn about Jesus. The week's work is to understand those choices and the relationship between written testimony, personal study, and revelation.

Required reading for every speaker

  • Genesis 1:1–5; Moses 1:31–33; 2:1–5.
  • Matthew 1:1–17; Mark 1:1–15; Luke 1:1–4; John 1:1–18.
  • John 20:24–31; 21:20–25; D&C 93:1–29.
  • 1 Nephi 13:20–41; 2 Nephi 29:6–14; Articles of Faith 1:8–9.
  • Joseph Smith—History 1:8–20; D&C 88:118–126.
  • Orson Pratt, *Divine Authenticity of the Book of Mormon*, no. 1 (1850), numbered paragraphs 1–8, under "To Expect More Revelation Is Not Unscriptural."
  • Brigham Young, "Learning by Study and by Faith," chapter 27.
  • Bible Dictionary entries "Gospels" and "John, Gospel of."
  • Matthew 14:13–21; Mark 6:30–44; Luke 9:10–17; John 6:1–15.
  • JST John 1:1–34 in the Bible appendix; compare with John 1.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. Compare the opening of John with Genesis 1 and D&C 93. What does each passage contribute to the claim that Jesus is the Word? How might that title affect the way a reader hears his teachings?
  2. What does Luke promise his reader in Luke 1:1–4? Compare his purpose with John's in John 20:30–31. How might those purposes affect the events each author includes?
  3. Choose an event recorded in two Gospels, such as the feeding of the multitude. What does each telling emphasize? What would be lost if a teacher replaced both accounts with a single combined version?
  4. Research the proposed dates and authorship of one Gospel using the Bible Dictionary and the book's own statements. Which conclusions come from the text and which depend on other evidence? How much does the interpretation of your chosen passage depend on those conclusions?
  5. Orson Pratt argues against treating the biblical collection as the end of revelation. Which parts of his argument address what scripture says, and which make historical claims? Does establishing the possibility of further revelation establish the truth of a particular new book?
  6. Brigham Young treats learning as a religious responsibility. What habits of study would follow from his teaching? Where can study and prayer help each other, and where might someone wrongly use one to avoid the other?
  7. In Joseph Smith—History, what turns a passage Joseph reads into a question he acts upon? Has studying a passage ever changed what you believed you should do? What made it consequential?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Choose a saying or action of Jesus that has shaped your understanding of him. How did you first learn it? What changes when you read it within the chapter where it appears?
  2. How much of what you believe about Christ have you read for yourself, and how much have you been told?
