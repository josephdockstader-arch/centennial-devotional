The Master

Fishers of Men

Ye call me Master and Lord: and ye say well; for so I am.

About this name

Jesus accepts the names Master and Lord after washing his disciples' feet. He then makes his action the basis of an instruction: they are to do for one another what he has done for them. His authority includes the right to call people into a different way of living and the example by which that life is taught.

The calling narratives introduce fishermen, a tax collector, and others whose associations with Jesus develop through particular encounters. Peter responds to a catch of fish by confessing his sinfulness. Nathanael begins with an objection about Nazareth. Their histories help us examine how trust develops and what people leave, receive, and become through discipleship.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. Compare Peter's encounters with Jesus in John 1 and Luke 5. What does each account contribute to the relationship? Why might the catch of fish lead Peter to ask Jesus to leave?
  2. Mark 1:16–20 mentions family and hired servants, and Luke 5:10 adds that James and John were partners with Simon. Research the organization of fishing work around Galilee. What might "leaving" involve for these disciples and the people connected to them?
  3. Jesus calls twelve and sends them with authority. Compare that choice with Exodus 19 and D&C 18. How does a calling connect an individual's work with a larger people and purpose?
  4. Why does Jesus defend eating with publicans and sinners in Luke 5? What objections to his choice of associates does his answer address? Could a religious community raise similar objections today?
  5. In John 13, Peter initially refuses to have his feet washed. Why might receiving service be difficult? What does Jesus's response suggest about the relationship between receiving from him and serving others?
  6. Compare the leadership described in D&C 121 with Joseph Smith's teachings. What gives a person moral influence beyond the authority of an office? What happens when the two are separated?
  7. Nathanael voices an objection before deciding to follow. What makes "come and see" an appropriate response? When is an invitation to experience something more helpful than an argument?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. What has discipleship asked you to leave, change, or take responsibility for? How would you distinguish a necessary sacrifice from a burden someone has mistakenly added to the gospel?
  2. He washed their feet and told them to do the same for one another. Who is the person you would least want to do that for? What is behind that?
