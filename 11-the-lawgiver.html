The Lawgiver

The Sermon on the Mount

For the Lord is our judge, the Lord is our lawgiver, the Lord is our king; he will save us.

Isaiah 33:22

About this name

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus teaches about anger, desire, promises, retaliation, generosity, prayer, and judgment. He speaks with authority while declaring that he has come to fulfill the law and the prophets. The listeners must decide what obedience requires when his teaching reaches the motives and relationships behind an outward act.

The sermon also describes the Father: he gives to those who ask, knows what his children need, and sends rain on both the just and the unjust. Those descriptions help explain the conduct Jesus commands. Reading the sermon beside 3 Nephi reveals how similar teachings address another covenant community and how the risen Lord speaks of the law's fulfillment.

Required reading for every speaker

  • Matthew 5–7; Isaiah 33:22.
  • Exodus 19:1–8; 20:1–21; Leviticus 19:9–18, 33–34.
  • 3 Nephi 12–14; 15:1–10.
  • Galatians 5:13–26; Moroni 7:5–13, 43–48.
  • D&C 42:18–29, 38–42; Moses 6:57–60.
  • Brigham Young, "Developing Christlike Attitudes toward Others," chapter 30.
  • Luke 6:20–49; James 1:19–27; 2:1–13.
  • John Taylor, "Love Thy Neighbour as Thyself," chapter 3.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. Who is blessed in Matthew 5:3–12, and what are they promised? How does this opening prepare readers for the demands that follow? Which blessing is most difficult to understand as a blessing?
  2. Compare the six "but I say unto you" passages in Matthew 5:21–48. Is Jesus intensifying a command, addressing its interpretation, exposing its purpose, or doing something else? Test your explanation against more than one passage.
  3. Read the commands concerning retaliation and love beside Leviticus 19. What continuities do you find? What does Jesus ask of his hearers when he includes enemies among those they must love?
  4. Matthew 6 repeatedly addresses acts performed to be seen. Can an act benefit someone and still be spiritually compromised by its motive? How do Moroni 7 and the sermon help examine that possibility?
  5. Compare the wording and setting of Matthew 5:48 and 3 Nephi 12:48. Research the meaning of "perfect" in Matthew, then consider the examples immediately preceding it. What kind of completeness is being asked for?
  6. How can "judge not" in Matthew 7 be read alongside the instruction to recognize false prophets by their fruits? What distinguishes discernment from the judgment Jesus condemns?
  7. Brigham Young discusses treatment of other people in concrete social situations. Choose one of his teachings and compare it with a passage from the sermon. What would acting on both require during an actual disagreement?
  8. The sermon ends with two builders. Why does Jesus make doing, rather than simply hearing, the decisive difference? What would count as building on his teaching over time?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Which command in Matthew 5–7 would most change your conduct at school, at home, or with friends? What makes it difficult? How does the sermon's account of the Father affect your willingness to attempt it?
  2. Choose one command from Matthew 5–7 that you do not keep. Say plainly why you do not keep it.
