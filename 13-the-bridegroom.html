The Bridegroom

The Parables on the Road and in the Temple

And at midnight there was a cry made, Behold, the bridegroom cometh; go ye out to meet him.

Matthew 25:6

About this name

When asked why his disciples do not fast, Jesus describes himself as a bridegroom whose presence gives his companions reason to rejoice. He also anticipates a time when he will be taken away. In Matthew 25, the bridegroom's arrival requires preparation through an unexpected delay.

The parables assigned here place questions about readiness beside questions about mercy. A father receives a returning son; another son objects. A servant receives the cancellation of a debt and then refuses mercy to someone else. A Samaritan assists a wounded stranger. These stories ask what it means to receive God's generosity and what that reception should make possible in our dealings with other people.

Required reading for every speaker

  • Matthew 9:14–17; 18:21–35; 22:1–14; 25:1–13.
  • Luke 10:25–37; 15; 16:19–31.
  • Isaiah 54:4–10; D&C 33:17–18; 45:56–59.
  • Mosiah 4:11–27; Alma 34:28–34; Moroni 7:43–48.
  • John Taylor, "Love Thy Neighbour as Thyself," chapter 3.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. In Matthew 9, why is a wedding an answer to a question about fasting? What does the bridegroom image suggest about the disciples' relationship with Jesus and the changes they will face?
  2. All ten virgins sleep in Matthew 25. What, then, distinguishes readiness from unreadiness? Compare the story with D&C 45:56–59. What can people help one another develop, and what cannot simply be transferred at the last moment?
  3. Identify the complaint that introduces Luke 15. How does each story answer it? Why might Jesus end with the older son's objection rather than the younger son's return?
  4. Which son's position is easier for you to understand? What is fair or unfair from his point of view? How does the father's answer change the terms of the disagreement?
  5. Research the scale of the two debts in Matthew 18 using a daily wage as a point of comparison. What does the size of the difference do to the story? What does the story suggest about the connection between receiving mercy and extending it?
  6. Compare the lawyer's question with Jesus's final question in Luke 10. What changes in the way the problem of neighborliness is framed? How does Taylor's treatment of love help you examine that change?
  7. The rich man asks for someone to warn his brothers. What does Abraham's answer suggest about the relationship between evidence and repentance? Compare it with the instructions about the poor in Mosiah 4.
  8. What makes forgiveness difficult when the injury is real? How can Matthew 18 guide a response while leaving room for accountability and protection from further harm?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Which of these parables most changes your understanding of being welcomed by Christ? What response does that welcome ask of you toward someone else?
  2. Who has asked your forgiveness and not received it? What are you waiting for?

Additional research readings

  • Luke 7:36–50; D&C 64:7–14; Hosea 2:14–23.
  • Joseph Smith, "Charity, the Pure Love of Christ," chapter 37.
