The Resurrection and the Life

Lazarus, Bethany, and the Road to Jerusalem

Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live.

John 11:25

About this name

Martha tells Jesus that Lazarus would not have died if he had been there. Jesus promises that her brother will rise, and she answers with her belief in the resurrection at the last day. His reply locates that hope in himself: "I am the resurrection, and the life." Martha responds by confessing him as the Christ and Son of God.

John then records Mary's grief, Jesus's tears, and Lazarus's emergence from the tomb. The event produces belief among some witnesses and a decision to kill Jesus among the chief priests and Pharisees. The readings follow those consequences through Bethany and the entry into Jerusalem. They also place the sisters' loss beside Restoration teachings about death, resurrection, and consolation.

Required reading for every speaker

  • John 11; 12:1–19; Luke 10:38–42.
  • Matthew 21:1–17; Zechariah 9:9–12; Psalm 118:19–29.
  • Alma 40:1–26; Mosiah 16:6–9.
  • D&C 42:45–46; Moses 7:44–47, 55–57.
  • Joseph Smith, "Words of Hope and Consolation at the Time of Death," chapter 14.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. What does Martha already believe before Jesus says, "I am the resurrection, and the life"? What does his reply ask her to understand about him? Why might John place her confession before Lazarus is raised?
  2. Mary and Martha make the same statement about Jesus's absence. How does he respond to each sister? What might those differences teach about responding to people who are grieving?
  3. Jesus knows Lazarus will rise, yet he weeps. Compare this with D&C 42:45–46 and Joseph Smith's teachings. What place does mourning have in a life that includes confidence in resurrection?
  4. John gives several explanations for the delay and the miracle. How do those explanations look from the perspective of Jesus, the disciples, and the sisters? What remains difficult about waiting even when an eventual purpose is understood?
  5. Compare Lazarus's return with Alma 40's account of resurrection. What makes being restored to mortal life different from receiving an immortal body? What does the distinction do to Christ's promise?
  6. Follow the discussion in John 11:45–53. What do the leaders fear, and how does John interpret Caiaphas's proposal? How can the same event become grounds for faith and for opposition?
  7. Research the royal imagery in Zechariah 9 and Psalm 118, then read the entry into Jerusalem. What kind of king is being presented? How does that presentation compare with the fears voiced after Lazarus's raising?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Which response to grief in these readings would you find most helpful, and why? What might you learn from a classmate who chooses a different response?
  2. If the resurrection is true, what should be different about a funeral? What have you seen that was different, and what was not?

Additional research readings

  • 1 Corinthians 15:20–26, 42–55; Luke 7:11–17; Mark 5:21–43.
  • Brigham Young, "Understanding Death and Resurrection," chapter 37.
