The Light of the World

He Is Risen

But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept.

1 Corinthians 15:20

About this name

Jesus calls himself the light of the world and promises his followers the light of life. After the destruction recorded in 3 Nephi, a voice out of the darkness says it again: “I am the light and the life of the world” (3 Nephi 9:18). That is Christ speaking before the Resurrection, while the darkness still covers the land. He appears at Bountiful afterward and identifies himself in person (3 Nephi 11:10–11). His Resurrection gives this title a particular force: the one who died now stands before witnesses, speaks to them, and invites them to recognize him.

Paul calls the risen Christ the firstfruits of those who have died. The harvest image connects his Resurrection with the resurrection promised to others. John and Luke describe the encounters through which disciples come to believe; Doctrine and Covenants 138 extends the study to the redemption of the dead. This cohort considers what resurrection means for human bodies, relationships, and hope.

This cohort is intended for the week after Easter, with the date to be confirmed in the schedule.

Required reading for every speaker

  • John 8:12; 20–21; Luke 24; Matthew 28:1–10; Mark 16:1–8.
  • Leviticus 23:9–14; 1 Corinthians 15.
  • 3 Nephi 9:15–18; 11:1–17; Alma 11:40–45.
  • 1 Peter 3:18–20; 4:6; D&C 88:6–16.
  • Moses 7:55–57.
  • Brigham Young, "Understanding Death and Resurrection," chapter 37.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. Research the firstfruits offering in Leviticus 23. How does Paul use the relation between the first portion and the coming harvest to explain Christ's Resurrection? What assurance does the image offer?
  2. Compare Mary's recognition of Jesus, the recognition at Emmaus, and Thomas's encounter. What brings recognition in each case? What does each story suggest about the relationship among testimony, experience, and belief?
  3. Matthew 28, Mark 16, Luke 24 and John 20 all describe the morning at the tomb, and they do not agree about who went, what they saw, or what they were told. Pick one of those disagreements and find out what has been proposed to account for it. What do you make of the explanations you find?
  4. Why does Paul say that faith is vain if Christ has not risen? What would remain of his message without resurrection, and why does he regard that as insufficient?
  5. Compare the body described in Luke 24 and John 20 with Alma 11 and D&C 88. What continuity and change do these passages describe between mortal and resurrected life? How does Brigham Young develop the subject?
  6. 1 Peter 3:18–20 and 4:6 say the gospel was preached to the dead. What does the passage itself establish, and what does it leave open? What would you want to know before building much on it?
  7. Compare Christ's identification as light and life in John 8, 3 Nephi 9, and D&C 88. How does resurrection help explain the title? What does following that light ask of someone who believes death is not the end?
  8. Peter meets the risen Jesus after having denied him. How does John 21 connect love, forgiveness, and future responsibility? What does the encounter suggest about beginning again after failure?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. What difference does the promise of resurrection make to the way you regard your body or another person's body? Which passage most influences your answer?
  2. Peter denied him and was given work to do afterward. What failure of your own do you still treat as final?

Additional research readings

  • Parley P. Pratt, Key to the Science of Theology (1855), chapter XV, on resurrection and degrees of glory.
  • John 11:7–16 alongside Thomas's encounter in John 20.
  • D&C 76:50–113; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18.
