Alpha and Omega

This Same Jesus

About this name

Alpha and Omega are the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet. Revelation places the title beside language about beginning and ending and the Lord's presence across time. Doctrine and Covenants 19 identifies the speaker as Jesus Christ and joins that title to his accomplishment of the Father's will. Revelation gives the title both to God and to Christ, and this program does not treat that as a problem to be solved, because in this reading God names an office. Where Christ speaks as the first and the last, he speaks in the office he holds under the Father, and in section 19 he says as much in his own voice.

At the Ascension, the disciples are promised that the same Jesus who has gone from them will return. The year ends by studying that promise alongside witnesses of the risen Lord, including Stephen, Paul, Joseph Smith, and Oliver Cowdery. The readings also ask what preparation means while the promise remains unfulfilled: how people are to serve, endure, and order their lives in expectation of his coming.

  • D&C 29:9–13; 65:1–6; Moses 7:58–67.
  • Matthew 25:14–46; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–5:11.

Questions for research and discussion

  1. What does "this same Jesus" contribute to the promise in Acts 1? Which encounter studied during the year most influences what you hope for or expect from his return?
  2. Compare Stephen's, Paul's, and Joseph Smith's encounters with the Lord. What circumstances precede each encounter, and what follows it? How does each witness become responsible for more than a private experience?
  3. Paul places his experience among the appearances of the risen Christ in 1 Corinthians 15. How does he explain his place in that company? What relationship between grace and responsibility does his account describe?
  4. Compare Matthew 24 with Joseph Smith—Matthew. How does the latter distinguish events concerning Jerusalem from events concerning the Son of Man's coming? How does that distinction affect the interpretation of "this generation"?
  5. What objection is raised in 2 Peter 3, and how does the writer answer it? How does patience differ from concluding that a promise will never be fulfilled?
  6. Examine Joseph Smith's teachings about preparation and the timing of the Second Coming. Compare them with Matthew 24:36–51 and D&C 45. Which responsibilities are clear even when the date is unknown?
  7. Revelation 21 describes God dwelling with his people and the end of death and sorrow. How does that vision connect with Emmanuel and the Resurrection? What concerns or longings does it address for you?
  8. If hope in Christ's return is to influence the coming summer, what difference could it make in a relationship, responsibility, or decision? Which of the assigned texts would guide that choice?

Questions for reflection

These are for the speaker, not for the meeting. Write your answers before the discussion or while you are drafting. Nobody will collect them.

  1. Which title of Christ has become more meaningful to you during the year? Identify a passage that changed your understanding and explain what you now see in it.
  2. If you were told he would come this summer, what would you do differently? What is keeping you from doing it now?
