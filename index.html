The Life of Jesus Christ

Vivit · He lives

Why the year is built this way

Two years ago our devotionals were built on single words out of scripture. Behold. Remember. Be still. Last year we walked the life of the Prophet Joseph Smith in order, from the grove to Carthage, and by May this student body had heard his whole life told back to it. This year, at Brother Hammon's direction, we take up the life of the Savior.

That is a harder assignment than it sounds, and you should know why before you start.

Joseph Smith left journals, letters, sermons, and a life that historians can date to the week. The Gospels give us something else. Four men wrote four accounts, none of them a biography in the way we use the word. Thirty years of the Savior's life are disposed of in a single verse. His last week takes up a third of the pages. Two of the writers say nothing about his birth at all. And his mission does not begin in Bethlehem or end at the empty tomb, which means any honest treatment of his life has to reach back before the world was made and forward past our own day.

So this year is organized by his names.

Each cohort is named for a title of Christ, and each takes the stretch of his life where that title does its work. The Carpenter's Son is in Nazareth. The Lawgiver is on the mount. The Lamb of God is at the cross. The Good Shepherd is at Bountiful, speaking to sheep of another fold. The title tells you what your week is about, and your first talk of the week will open it.

There are twenty-three cohorts, running from the second week of October to the end of April. They move in order, from the council held before this world existed to the promise that he will come again. Christmas falls in the week we speak of his birth. Good Friday and Easter fall in the weeks we speak of the cross and the empty tomb.

What your cohort asks of you

Within each cohort, every speaker holds a different seat, so that eight students can stand up in one week and say eight different things about a single moment.

  1. The Office What the cohort's title claims, and what that name required of him. This talk opens the week and gives the rest of us the frame.
  2. The Case Why the writer recorded this at all. What was he arguing, and who was he arguing it to?
  3. The Epistle What the apostles taught about this moment after it was over, and what it does to us.
  4. The Restoration What the Prophet Joseph Smith, the Book of Mormon, or the revelations add that the Bible alone does not carry.
  5. The Text Close work on the passage itself. What the words are doing, and what the Greek or Hebrew underneath them opens up.
  6. The World The setting rebuilt. Who governed, what the custom was, what a thing cost, what it would have looked like to stand there.
  7. The Witness One person who was there, and the distance between what they saw and what they understood.
  8. The Objection The hardest thing said against this moment, named plainly and answered.

Cohorts of six carry the Office, Restoration, Text, World, Witness, and Objection seats. Cohorts of eight add the Case and the Epistle. The two parable cohorts work differently, and their pages explain how.

The standard

Every talk in this program is about ten minutes and ends the same way, in the name of Jesus Christ, amen. Beyond that, one thing is asked of you above all others.

Do the research. The difference between a talk this school remembers and a talk it politely sits through is almost never the writing. It is whether the speaker went and found something. A real date, a real price, a real quote from someone who was standing there. When a draft feels thin or sentimental, the answer is not better sentences. The answer is to go back to the sources and come back with something the room does not already know.

Every cohort lists the passages each speaker in that group is expected to read before drafting, and a set of questions to work through together. Start there.
