The Life of Jesus Christ

Vivit · He lives

Why the year is built this way

The Father speaks rarely in the scriptures, and when he does, he often speaks of his Son. During the baptism at the Jordan he says, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” (Matt. 3:17). At the Mount of Transfiguration he says again, “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased,” and adds, “hear ye him” (Matt. 17:5). At Bountiful, the Nephites hear, “Behold my Beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased, in whom I have glorified my name—hear ye him” (3 Ne. 11:7). In the Sacred Grove, Joseph Smith hears, “This is My Beloved Son. Hear Him!” (JS—H 1:17). The pattern says something important about how God deals with his children: he makes himself known through his Son.

King Benjamin connects knowing Christ with knowing his name. After teaching his people about the atonement, he gives them the name of Christ as part of the covenant they have made: “Ye shall be called the children of Christ … there is no other name given whereby salvation cometh” (Mosiah 5:7–8). He then tells them to “retain the name written always in your hearts,” so that they may “hear and know the voice by which ye shall be called, and also, the name by which he shall call you” (Mosiah 5:12). His explanation ends with a question: “For how knoweth a man the master whom he has not served, and who is a stranger unto him, and is far from the thoughts and intents of his heart?” (Mosiah 5:13). Taking the name of Christ means something only if we come to know the person whose name we have taken.

Names often carry this kind of meaning in scripture. Abram becomes Abraham when God establishes his covenant with him. Jacob receives the name Israel after wrestling through the night. When Jesus first meets Simon, he tells him, “Thou shalt be called Cephas, which is by interpretation, A stone” (John 1:42). Peter receives the name before he has grown into it. In scripture, a name sometimes tells us who a person is before his life has finished showing it.

The names given to Jesus also tell us something about who he is and what he came to do. The angel tells Joseph, “Thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins” (Matt. 1:21). Isaiah calls him “Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace” (Isa. 9:6). John the Baptist sees him coming and says, “Behold the Lamb of God” (John 1:29). Jesus calls himself the “good shepherd” (John 10:11). After the resurrection, Thomas calls him “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28). Paul writes that God “hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name” (Philip. 2:9). The Doctrine and Covenants states that “Jesus Christ is the name which is given of the Father” (D&C 18:23).

These names provide the structure for this year’s devotional. The students are divided into twenty-three cohorts, each named for a scriptural name or title of Christ. The cohorts move roughly through the chronology of his life, but the names give another way of organizing the study. Lamb of God, Son of God, Beloved Son, Shepherd, Savior, Redeemer—each name carries meanings that reach beyond the passage where it appears. Some reach back into Israel’s sacrifices and worship. Others draw on family, kingship, prophecy, or familiar kinds of work. A shepherd was not an illustration to the people who first heard Jesus use the word. It was a person they knew. A lamb brought to the temple belonged to a form of worship they understood. A claim to kingship meant something in a country ruled by Rome. Learning the names of Christ requires learning something about the world in which those names were spoken.

The four Gospels encourage this kind of study. None gives a complete account of the life of Jesus. Eighteen years pass in a single verse in Luke (Luke 2:52), while the final week of his life fills a large portion of all four Gospels. Mark and John say nothing about his birth. John tells us why he chose what to include: “these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name” (John 20:31). He closes by saying that Jesus did many other things that were never written down (John 21:25). Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John chose the events they recorded because they were bearing witness of Jesus Christ.

Alongside the miracles and teachings, they preserve small details. John remembers six stone waterpots at the wedding in Cana and tells us they were there “after the manner of the purifying of the Jews” (John 2:6). Mark remembers that Jesus was asleep on a cushion in the back of the boat (Mark 4:38). The disciples calculate that two hundred pennyworth of bread would not feed the multitude (John 6:7). Judas complains that Mary’s ointment could have been sold for three hundred pence (John 12:5). The Gospels give the names of villages and rulers, tell us what people ate and what work they did, mention feast days and family relationships, and preserve details about Jewish worship and Roman government. Jesus lived his life among these things.

Understanding these details can change the way a familiar passage is read. Three hundred pence means more after learning what a laborer was ordinarily paid for a day’s work. The waterpots at Cana make more sense when their connection to purification is understood. “King of the Jews” means something different in a land ruled by Caesar. “Lamb of God” reaches back into centuries of sacrifice, Passover, and prophecy. The road between Galilee and Jerusalem crossed real country and passed through Samaria. The people who heard Jesus called the Messiah already had ideas about what the Messiah would be. Some recognized him. Others expected something different. Even those who followed him did not always understand him.

John writes that “the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). The Word became flesh in a particular place, among particular people, at a particular time. Christ was born into a family and a nation with a history, a religion, and hopes for what God would do for Israel. He went to synagogues and weddings. He ate in other people’s houses. He crossed the Sea of Galilee in fishing boats. He taught in the temple and along roadsides. His parables speak of wages, debts, fields, sheep, inheritance, marriage, household work, and lost coins because these belonged to the lives of the people listening to him. The world of the Gospels is not separate from the life of Christ. It is the world in which he chose to live his mortal life.

The life of Christ in scripture also reaches beyond the years between Bethlehem and Calvary. Prophets in the Old Testament looked forward to his coming. The Gospels record his birth, ministry, death, and resurrection. Third Nephi records his ministry among the Nephites. Joseph Smith records seeing him with the Father in the Sacred Grove. For this reason, Devotional begins before the creation of the world and ends with the Second Coming.

Last year’s devotional was built around the preservation of a sacred record. The life of Joseph Smith gave us journals, letters, revelations, meeting minutes, newspapers, and personal memories. Those records allowed us to follow his life closely and to hear from people who knew him. The record of Christ’s life asks for a somewhat different kind of work. It is much older. It comes to us through scripture written in another time, place, and language. There are things Matthew or John could assume their first readers understood that now require some study from us. History, geography, language, and archaeology can help fill in that world, but they serve the scriptural record rather than replace it. The purpose is to read the scriptures with greater understanding.

King Benjamin’s question remains at the center of the year: “For how knoweth a man the master … who is a stranger unto him?” (Mosiah 5:13). It is possible to know the teachings of Christ and still know very little about him. Faith in Christ asks more than knowing what he taught; it invites us to know him personally. The twenty-three names give us different ways to study the same life, and that study will require careful reading, good sources, attention to detail, and a willingness to keep looking when something is not yet understood. Those things are means rather than the end. The reason for studying the life of Jesus Christ is the one King Benjamin gave: to know the Master whose name we have taken.

By the end of the year, the stories should be more familiar. More importantly, so should Christ.

What your cohort asks of you

Within each cohort, every speaker holds a different seat, so that eight students can stand up in one week and say eight different things about a single moment.

  1. The Office What the cohort’s title claims, and what that name required of him. This talk opens the week and gives the rest of us the frame.
  2. The Case Why the writer recorded this at all. What was he arguing, and who was he arguing it to?
  3. The Epistle What the apostles taught about this moment after it was over, and what it does to us.
  4. The Restoration What the Prophet Joseph Smith, the Book of Mormon, or the revelations add that the Bible alone does not carry.
  5. The Text Close work on the passage itself. What the words are doing, and what the Greek or Hebrew underneath them opens up.
  6. The World The setting rebuilt. Who governed, what the custom was, what a thing cost, what it would have looked like to stand there.
  7. The Witness One person who was there, and the distance between what they saw and what they understood.
  8. The Objection The hardest thing said against this moment, named plainly and answered.

Cohorts of six carry the Office, Restoration, Text, World, Witness, and Objection seats. Cohorts of eight add the Case and the Epistle. The two parable cohorts work differently, and their pages explain how.

The standard

Every talk in this program is about ten minutes and ends the same way, in the name of Jesus Christ, amen. Beyond that, one thing is asked of you above all others.

Do the research. The difference between a talk this school remembers and a talk it politely sits through is almost never the writing. It is whether the speaker went and found something. A real date, a real price, a real quote from someone who was standing there. When a draft feels thin or sentimental, the answer is not better sentences. The answer is to go back to the sources and come back with something the room does not already know.

Every cohort lists the passages each speaker in that group is expected to read before drafting, and a set of questions to work through together. Start there.
